Index funds and corporate governance

Do the world's largest shareholders actually use the power that comes with the shares they hold?

Passive funds, principally BlackRock, Vanguard, and State Street, now hold an average combined stake of around 20% in most major American listed companies. Because they track an index rather than pick stocks, they can't sell out of a company they disagree with, which in theory should make them unusually attentive owners. This thesis asks whether that theoretical incentive shows up in practice, or whether the low-fee model that makes passive investing so attractive to savers also limits how seriously these funds can monitor the thousands of companies they own.

Approach

The starting point is a genuine puzzle in the academic literature: two papers using an almost identical method arrive at opposite conclusions about what passive ownership does to governance. Both take advantage of a quirk in how the Russell 1000 and Russell 2000 indices are assigned each year, which lets researchers compare companies of near-identical size that end up with very different levels of passive ownership purely because of which index they land in, a natural experiment that strips out the usual chicken-and-egg problem between good governance and investor interest.

Reconciling those two papers is the core of the thesis; the difference, it turns out, comes down to what each one chooses to measure. From there, I test the same question against a harder, more concrete outcome (corporate innovation) and then hold the theory up against BlackRock's own 2025 Investment Stewardship Report, to see whether the numbers a major passive manager publishes about itself support or complicate the academic picture.

What I found

Passive funds are capable of pushing through standardised, low-cost governance changes, such as backing more independent boards or removing anti-takeover provisions, but they fall short on the deeper, continuous monitoring that decisions like CEO oversight or long-term strategy actually require. BlackRock's own stewardship data tells a consistent story: real growth in resources and transparency since 2018, sitting alongside direct contact with only a small share of the companies it holds. The 2021 Exxon proxy vote is the case most people point to as proof that passive funds can move markets, and it does show that, but it is also a single, highly visible vote on a symbolic issue, cast through exactly the low-cost channel these funds are built to use well, not the everyday scrutiny the model still struggles to deliver.

The locked-in owners have the right incentives, at least in theory, but the structure of their operating costs and their conflicts of interest transforms those theoretical incentives into something much weaker in practice.
